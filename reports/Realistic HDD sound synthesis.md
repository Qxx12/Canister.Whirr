# Ringing Modes and a Virtual Head Fix Whirr

A real hard-drive seek is not a noise burst. It is a gesture lasting 10–40 ms. A sharp voice-coil current edge strikes a handful of fixed, fairly narrow structural resonances, mostly between 1 and 6 kHz. The result has an instantaneous attack, a 10 dB drop within 3–7 ms, and a tail at −9 to −16 dB that lasts another 20–40 ms. Long seeks add a second burst about 14–17 ms after the first, and many drives from 1993–2007 add a quiet, low-Q 190–300 Hz thump that peaks 6–14 ms after the click. Under load, a drive produces roughly 10–20 audible seek gestures per second with irregular, long-tailed gaps. A 7200 rpm drive doing random I/O cannot exceed about 80 seeks/s. Measured against this, Whirr v2's seven Q 3–8 bandpasses decay 60 dB in 2–14 ms. That is five to ten times faster than real drives, and it puts the sound below the damping range listeners hear as rubber or wood. Its kHz bands also die first, so the 0.5–1 kHz bands carry what little tail there is, the reverse of a real drive. Per-buffer peak normalization flattens the dynamics, and a 0.3 s-smoothed rate accumulator turns bursty disk counters into a near-metronome. That is why v2 sounds "flat". v1 sounded bassy for a different reason: it put a 170–300 Hz body mode into the drive's own instant ring, when the real low component is quiet, low-Q, delayed, and absent from modern drives. The fix has two parts. The first is a modal resonator bank struck by short contact pulses, organized into multi-burst seek gestures and normalized once per bank rather than per buffer. The second is a "virtual head" simulation that turns each 33 ms counter delta into seeks of chosen distance and spaces them by seek-curve time plus rotational wait, under a mechanical budget. The main caveat is that the decay and Q targets come from lossy, close-mic CC0 recordings with undocumented mounting, and no published source gives measured modal damping. The final step is therefore an A/B harness against those recordings.

## A seek strikes a few fixed kHz modes, twice

**The physics is settled at the level Whirr needs.** A voice-coil seek has four phases: speedup, coast (long seeks only), slowdown, and settle ([Ruemmler & Wilkes 1994](https://pages.cs.wisc.edu/~dusseau/Classes/CS537-F05/Readings/ruemmler94introduction.pdf)). Seek noise comes from the head actuator. It is "normally dominated by fewer tones" tied to the actuator's structural resonances, and those tones **stay fixed in frequency regardless of seek speed**. Level is set by seek speed, the coil-current profile, and actuator damping ([Jiang & Macioce, Roush](https://cdn.mediavalet.com/usva/roush/Rsrqc_YhbU-GP6sYr323gg/4WWZaqVt6EKK92EQL0k7_A/Original/Data-Storage.pdf)). Patents name the trigger: bang-bang control produces square current waveforms whose harmonics excite head-gimbal and structural resonances, and even smoothed profiles leave "jerks" at seek start and end that still radiate ([US6441988](https://patents.google.com/patent/US6441988); [US 7289290](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/7289290)). Arm modes ring "whenever the rotary actuated arm comes to a sudden stop" ([Roush](https://cdn.mediavalet.com/usva/roush/Rsrqc_YhbU-GP6sYr323gg/4WWZaqVt6EKK92EQL0k7_A/Original/Data-Storage.pdf)). Quiet-seek and AAM modes lower peak current and soften these edges without moving the mode frequencies. They cost about 10% in random access time ([Wikipedia: AAM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Automatic_acoustic_management)) and cut seek level by 6–8 dB: 49→43 dB on a Hitachi 7K1000.B ([Tom's Hardware](https://www.tomshardware.com/reviews/terabyte-hitachi-acoustic,2084.html)) and 3.8→3.0 B on a 2005 drive ([SPCR](https://silentpcreview.com/article244-page1.html)). That is direct evidence that the "clicky" character comes from sharp excitation edges, not from the resonators. Literature frequencies are consistent but thin. One IEEE study shows new seek peaks near **4.5 and 5.5 kHz** (actuator sway/torsion) over 2–6 kHz cover content ([IEEE Trans. Magn., via ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/224607928_Numerical_Hard_Disk_Drive_Seek_Acoustic_Transfer_Analysis_and_Experimental_Validation)). Butterfly modes sit near 4.8–6 kHz in desktop designs ([US7298592](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/7298592); [US7085108](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/7085108)) and near 9 kHz in server actuators ([Toshiba Review](https://toshiba.semicon-storage.com/content/dam/toshiba-ss-v3/master/en/storage/technology-center/ToshibaReview_vol66n11.pdf)). These came from abstracts and search excerpts, not full texts. **No source found gives measured Q or T60 values for HDD modes.**

**Seeks are quiet on average and loud at their peaks.** Seagate's 7200.14 manual lists seek at only **+0.1 to +0.2 B (1–2 dB)** over idle sound power ([Seagate Desktop HDD manual](https://www.seagate.com/www-content/product-content/barracuda-fam/desktop-hdd/barracuda-7200-14/en-us/docs/100686584u.pdf)). A mid-2000s drive without AAM measured **+9 dB** ([SPCR](https://silentpcreview.com/article244-page1.html)). The CC0 recordings explain how a +2 dB average can still be clearly audible. Block RMS during seek activity rises only **+2 to +7 dB** over idle, while individual event peaks stand a median **13–16 dB** (p90 15–26 dB) above the idle envelope ([Klerrp "Old HDDs" pack, CC0](https://freesound.org/people/Klerrp/packs/8850/)). Perceived activity comes from crest factor, not loudness.

### What the recordings measure

The best evidence is the research team's own analysis of 18 openly licensed recordings: Welch "event-minus-idle" excess spectra, 0.25–1 ms band envelopes, and onset detection at +10 to +15 dB over the idle envelope. The core set is a CC0 Freesound pack of 13 close-mic drives from 1993–2012 with model numbers and rpm. All sources are lossy previews with unknown microphones and mounting, so the numbers below are cross-drive ranges, not specifications. Idle peaks land exactly at rpm/60 in every drive with clean idle: 60 Hz at 3600 rpm, 90 Hz at 5400, 120 Hz at 7200, 166 Hz at 10k ([155882](https://freesound.org/people/Klerrp/sounds/155882/), [166638](https://freesound.org/people/Klerrp/sounds/166638/), [166641](https://freesound.org/people/Klerrp/sounds/166641/), [182366](https://freesound.org/people/Klerrp/sounds/182366/)). That match validates the method.

Four regularities stand out (measured). **First, the energy is tonal and drive-specific.** Each drive has 3–8 irregularly spaced peaks, with spectral Q around 15–35. Older and cheaper drives centre near 1–3 kHz, and 2012 drives move up to 3.7–4 kHz with almost nothing below 2 kHz. **Second, the envelope has two stages:** an instantaneous attack (the 1–5 kHz and 5–16 kHz bands peak 0–4 ms after onset), a first 10 dB drop in 3–7 ms, then a tail at −9 to −16 dB that persists through the 30 ms window. "Grindier" drives drop more slowly, reaching −10 dB only after 18–43 ms. Median broadband T60 is **23–49 ms** for most drives and about 112 ms for the Samsung T166 and Maxtor Atlas. **Third, the strongest seeks have more than one burst.** In the Seagate clip, a first burst is followed 14–17 ms later by a second burst 2–5 dB weaker, with a hiss bed at −10 to −17 dB between them. Merging gaps up to 15 ms yields "seek units" with a median length of 12 ms (p90 41 ms) ([Commons Seagate](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Seagate_ST3250823A_Kopfger%C3%A4usche.ogg)). That spacing matches the move time of a long seek on a 21 ms full-stroke drive, which supports the physics reading of separate start and stop strikes (inference). **Fourth, the low component is real but secondary.** It appears in about half the drives, centred at 190–300 Hz, with low Q (WD102AA's 281 Hz peak has Q≈5) ([Commons WD102AA](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:WD102AA_Scratched_area_read_retries.ogg)). It peaks 6–14 ms after the click and, A-weighted, usually sits 5–16 dB below it. The 2012 drives and the Seagate clip have essentially none.

The spectral Q values understate how long modes ring. This conclusion is our own arithmetic, not a measurement in the notes. For a two-pole resonator, T60 = 6.91·Q/(πf). The WD click-of-death mode at 1.125 kHz shows a spectral Q of only about 22, yet it rings with T60 ≈ 130–220 ms ([Commons WD click](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:WD_bad_heads_click_of_death.ogg)), which implies a decay Q of about 66–112. Averaged spectra blur hit-to-hit frequency jitter into apparent bandwidth. Applying the same conversion to the healthy seeks, a 3–7 ms first −10 dB drop at 2.6 kHz corresponds to Q ≈ 20–50. A tail that keeps a broadband T60 of 25–50 ms needs one or two modes with Q ≈ 60–130. The two-stage envelope is therefore best read as two mode populations: a moderately damped cluster that makes the attack, plus a few lightly damped partials that make the tail. Listeners' descriptions fit this split: a "metallic ting" in 3.5-inch drives, and loud drives heard as "woosh+klonk" rather than a single click. One Hackaday commenter specifically criticized a piezo HDD clicker as too high in pitch with pulses that are too short ([Hackaday](https://hackaday.com/2022/09/26/tiny-dongle-brings-the-hard-drives-song-back-to-updated-retrocomputers)).

The thump is probably as much about mounting as about the drive. A drive suspended in rubber bands was about 12 dBA quieter on seeks than a hard-mounted one ([HWCooling](https://www.hwcooling.net/en/?p=7404)), and Seagate itself notes a desk can "act like a speaker" ([Seagate KB](https://www.seagate.com/support/kb/identifying-hard-drive-sounds-and-determining-what-they-mean/)). The 190–300 Hz component in close-mic recordings of bare drives on a surface is therefore plausibly reaction-torque energy radiated by the desk or case (inference). That makes it a preset choice, not part of the drive's core voice.

## Busy drives tick 10–20 times a second, in mechanically spaced bursts

**Seek time follows a known curve.** Short seeks take time proportional to √distance plus settle. Long seeks take time proportional to distance plus a constant. Seeks shorter than 2–4 cylinders are dominated by a 1–3 ms settle ([Ruemmler & Wilkes 1994](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~natassa/courses/15-721/papers/IEEEComputer.DiskModel.pdf)). A 2010s 7200 rpm drive needs **1.0 ms track-to-track, 8.5 ms average (9.5 ms write) and 4.16 ms average rotational latency** ([Seagate 7200.12 manual](https://www.seagate.com/staticfiles/support/disc/manuals/desktop/Barracuda%207200.12/100529369h.pdf)). WD's 2006-era 7200 rpm drives list a **21 ms full stroke** ([WD datasheet](https://images10.newegg.com/UploadFilesForNewegg/itemintelligence/WD/22-144-701/WD2500KS.pdf)). Random I/O therefore saturates at 1000/(8.5 + 4.17) ≈ **79 seeks/s**, consistent with the commonly quoted 75–100 IOPS for 7.2k SATA ([IOPS calculator](https://pcserverandparts.com/iops-calculator/)). Early-1990s drives were 1.5–4× slower. An HP C2200A followed t = 3.45 + 0.597√d ms up to 616 cylinders and 10.8 + 0.012d beyond ([Ruemmler & Wilkes](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~natassa/courses/15-721/papers/IEEEComputer.DiskModel.pdf)). 86Box's era presets run from 30 ms full stroke, 6 ms track-to-track at 3600 rpm (1992) to 15 ms, 2 ms at 7200 rpm (2000) ([86Box hdd.c](https://github.com/86Box/86Box/blob/master/src/disk/hdd.c)). A vintage drive therefore caps near 35–40 seeks/s (inference), and each of its seek gestures is longer and more distinct.

**The recordings show what "busy" sounds like.** In "writing" passages, drives produce **11–19 detected events/s** with long-tailed inter-onset intervals (IOIs). The Fujitsu's median is 32 ms (p10 16, p90 82). The WD JD is 33 ms (6/144), the Samsung SV1022D 39 ms (10/119), and the Samsung T166 34 ms (8/199) ([166638](https://freesound.org/people/Klerrp/sounds/166638/), [166898](https://freesound.org/people/Klerrp/sounds/166898/), [167420](https://freesound.org/people/Klerrp/sounds/167420/), [167421](https://freesound.org/people/Klerrp/sounds/167421/)). The Seagate random-access "crunch" shows **43 fine bursts/s (median IOI 15 ms, p10 5 ms) but only 12.9 merged seek units/s** ([Commons Seagate](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Seagate_ST3250823A_Kopfger%C3%A4usche.ogg)). What a listener counts as one "seek" is thus a cluster of about three bursts: start, stop and settle. Synthetic test patterns sound different. The Conner "seek test" is strictly periodic at 58 ms ([155882](https://freesound.org/people/Klerrp/sounds/155882/)), and WD102AA read retries run about 16/s with a regular 16 ms median ([Commons WD102AA](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:WD102AA_Scratched_area_read_retries.ogg)). That regularity is the sound of a fault or a benchmark, not normal use.

**Workload shapes the rhythm through seek distance and burstiness.** No source pairs workloads with recordings, so the following comes from mechanics (inference). Sequential transfers make almost no real seeks, only quiet track-switch ticks: on 86Box's "2000" geometry (about 1 MiB per cylinder), 60 MB/s crosses about 60 cylinders/s. 86Box itself plays a seek sound on every cylinder change, sequential ones included ([86Box hdd.c](https://github.com/86Box/86Box/blob/master/src/disk/hdd.c)). Random small I/O runs back to back at the IOPS ceiling, with gaps of seek time plus a uniform rotational wait, which gives a dense, irregular grind with no silences. A single-disk file copy alternates long source/destination seek pairs every chunk, a periodic "chk…chk". Journal and metadata syncs produce A→B→A long-seek pairs. Real traffic is bursty and spatially local: short seeks are "much more common" than the independent-request model predicts ([Ruemmler & Wilkes](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~natassa/courses/15-721/papers/IEEEComputer.DiskModel.pdf)), and a secondary summary attributes "a high degree of burstiness" to HP-UX disk traces ([Ruemmler & Wilkes 1993](https://www.cs.fsu.edu/~awang/courses/cop5611_s2017/hpux.pdf); full text not retrieved). NCQ reorders queued commands to minimize head travel ([TechSpot](https://www.techspot.com/news/16561-seagate-barracuda-72007-ncq-hard-drive-reviewed.html)). Under queueing, a burst therefore becomes a same-direction sweep of soft short seeks closed by one long return (inference). Idle is not silent either. WD Green and Red drives unload their heads after **8 s** by default ([NETGEAR community](https://community.netgear.com/t5/ReadyNAS-Hardware-Compatibility/WD-Red-drives-park-their-heads-like-WD-Green-drives/td-p/869565)), and older drives ran periodic thermal recalibration ([fedora-list FAQ](https://listman.redhat.com/archives/fedora-list/2004-December/msg06332.html)). The one shipping precedent for timing, 86Box, gets its rhythm from a timing model. It spaces sounds by simulated seek, rotation and transfer time, rate-limits onsets to one per track-to-track time, and mixes up to 8 voices ([86Box hdd_audio.c](https://github.com/86Box/86Box/blob/master/src/disk/hdd_audio.c)).

## Whirr v2 decays five to ten times too fast; v1 rang in the wrong register

The diagnosis comes from reading `Sources/Whirr/SoundBank.swift` and `Sonifier.swift` against the measurements above. Each v2 impact is white noise with a 0.15–0.5 ms exponential envelope, fed through seven RBJ bandpasses. The bands sit at 420–560 Hz (Q 3), 0.8–1 kHz (Q 4), 1.4–1.8 kHz (Q 6), 2.3–2.9 kHz (Q 7), 3.4–4.2 kHz (Q 8), 5–6.2 kHz (Q 8) and 6.8–8 kHz (Q 6). Applying T60 = 6.91·Q/(πf) gives the following, which is derived arithmetic rather than a measurement.

| v2 band (centre, Q) | T60 of band ring | Measured target for that region |
|---|---|---|
| 490 Hz, Q 3 | 13.5 ms | low-Q thump in some drives, but delayed and weak |
| 900 Hz, Q 4 | 9.8 ms | 1–5 kHz tail at −9 to −16 dB for 20–40 ms |
| 1.6 kHz, Q 6 | 8.2 ms | same |
| 2.6 kHz, Q 7 | 5.9 ms | broadband T60 23–49 ms; Seagate main peak here |
| 3.8 kHz, Q 8 | 4.6 ms | 2012 drives' main peak here |
| 5.6 kHz, Q 8 | 3.1 ms | WD JD keeps 28% of A-weighted energy above 5 kHz |
| 7.4 kHz, Q 6 | 1.8 ms | — |

Three defects follow. First, **every band dies within 2–14 ms, so the event is essentially the shape of its own noise envelope**, a 1–3 ms "tsk" with no partials decaying at their own rates. In Klatzky, Pai and Krotkov's material model, the decay constant τ_d = Q/π is the main material cue. The 50% category boundaries are τ_d ≈ 2.9 for rubber and 3.2 for wood, against 126 for glass and 450 for steel ([Klatzky, Pai & Krotkov 2000](https://www.cs.ubc.ca/labs/lci/papers/docs2000/pai-KlaPai00.pdf)). v2's τ_d of 1–2.5 sits below even rubber. Jointed metal structures have loss factors η ≈ 0.03–0.07 ([COMSOL](https://www.comsol.com/blogs/damping-in-structural-dynamics-theory-and-sources/?setlang=1)), equivalent to Q ≈ 15–33, so a screwed-together aluminium drive should land around τ_d ≈ 5–40. Giordano and McAdams found damping a weaker cue than frequency with real plates ([Giordano & McAdams 2006](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/68504)). The counterargument therefore deserves weight, but v2 is also wrong on frequency structure, so it fails on both cues. Second, **the decay order is inverted.** Constant-ish Q means the sub-kHz bands ring longest, so whatever tail v2 has is a dull 0.5–1 kHz smear. Real drives put their tail in the 1–5 kHz modes. Third, the per-hit detune of ±4% per band is harmless at Q 3–8 but would sound like a different drive on each hit once the modes are narrow. Same-object variation should stay near ±1% globally (inference). The ±10% pitch rule common in game audio ([Uppsala game-audio notes](https://babel.speldesign.uu.se/2018/03/01/2018-03-01-maximizing-your-audio-budget-using-pitch-variation/)) suits varied sources, not one physical drive.

The level handling compounds the problem. Each buffer is scaled to a fixed peak (0.35, 0.55 or 0.8 by kind) times 0.85–1.0, and playback adds a gain of 0.55–1.0 by intensity times 0.85–1.1. Within a burst, hits therefore vary by only about ±1 dB around three discrete levels. Real drives spread 2–13 dB between their p10 and p90 events ([Klerrp pack](https://freesound.org/people/Klerrp/packs/8850/)). There is also no bed under the clicks, so each lands on digital silence, and no early reflections, both of which push synthesized clicks toward "artificial" (inference). The 300 Hz highpass is sensible for a bare drive, but it also removes v2's own 140–240 Hz thump band almost entirely.

v1 failed the other way. Four damped sinusoids give four sparse partials, which read as a pitched woodblock: van den Doel and Pai's modal framework needs many more partials with frequency-proportional damping before material identity emerges ([van den Doel & Pai](https://persianney.com/kvdoelcsubc/publications/modalpaper.pdf)). Its 170–300 Hz "body" also sat inside the drive's instant ring. The measured low component is low-Q (Q≈4–5), arrives 6–14 ms late, is usually 5–16 dB below the click A-weighted, and is absent in the 2012 drives and the Seagate clip. Bass at onset and at click level matches no recording. The exact v1 Qs and gains are not in the current source, so this diagnosis rests on the user's description.

**The timing model is not actually Poisson, and that matters.** `Sonifier.update` adds `rate·dt` to a budget and emits one event per whole unit, each placed uniformly within the next 33 ms. The rate is driven by a 0.3 s exponential average of ops above the learned floor. At about 30 seeks/s this yields one event per tick, and the IOI is the difference of two uniform placements one tick apart. That gives a triangular distribution on 0–66 ms: median 33, p10 15, p90 51 ms (derived). Real writing passages have similar medians but p90 values of 82–199 ms, so v2's p90/median ratio is 1.5 against 2.6–5.9 in the recordings. At 15/s the accumulator fires every second or third tick, which is close to a jittered metronome. At the 80/s cap, about 2.6 events land in each tick, which allows sub-millisecond gaps that no drive can produce: the measured p10 is 5 ms even in the densest crunch. The 0.3 s smoothing also erases the burst structure that the 30 Hz counters do carry. A single 33 ms tick with 50 ops (1,500 ops/s instantaneously) moves the smoothed rate only about 10% of the way, so short bursts fall under the floor-plus-margin threshold and vanish. Those bursts are exactly the sparse "housekeeping" ticks that make a real idle drive feel alive. Finally, seek kind and gain are drawn independently per event, so nothing produces accents, sweeps or A→B→A pairs. A Poisson process would have fixed the regularity but not the rest: at 20/s an exponential IOI has median 35 ms and p90 115 ms, close to the recordings, but its p10 of 5 ms (15% of the median) still crowds events closer together than seek mechanics allow.

## Redesign part 1: strike a modal bank with a seek gesture

The synthesis should model the measured structure directly. A seek of normalized stroke distance d becomes a gesture of contact strikes at times set by the seek curve, with coast hiss between them. The strikes drive a resonator bank that has a moderately damped cluster and a few ringing partials. An optional delayed thump, an optional bed and a short early-reflection space complete the picture. Pre-rendering remains the right architecture, and render work stays off the audio thread. Pre-render gestures in **8 distance bins × 12–16 variants**, about 128 buffers of roughly 100 ms at 48 kHz. That is about 2.5 MB in total and renders in well under a second offline (derived).

In the tables below, "M" marks values measured in the recordings, "L" values from literature or datasheets, and "I" inferences to tune by ear.

**Gesture timeline.** Let T(d) be the preset's seek time (next section), t_s the settle time (1 ms for modern presets, 2–3 ms for vintage), and T_move = max(0, T(d) − t_s).

| Element | Timing | Level (re strike A) | Basis |
|---|---|---|---|
| Strike A (accel onset) | t = 0 | 0 dB reference | L (current-edge "jerk") |
| Strike B (accel→decel reversal) | T_move/2 for d < 0.33. For coast seeks, two weak strikes at end of accel and start of decel | −8 to −14 dB, default −10. Merged into A for T_move < 2 ms. Can be disabled | I (largest current step, but audibility unverified) |
| Coast/VCM hiss | Raised-cosine envelope across [A, C], only when T_move > 4 ms | −12 to −17 dB re burst peak, band 1–4 kHz, fed through the same bank | M (Seagate bed at −10 to −17 dB) |
| Strike C (arrival/stop) | t = T_move | −2 dB default, range −5 to +2 dB. Uses its own gain vector | M (second burst −2 to −5 dB at 14–17 ms); physics suggests the stop may be louder |
| Settle ticks | 1–3 strikes at C + 0.4–3 ms, geometric spacing ±20% | −8 to −16 dB re C | I (closed-loop settle; 1–3 ms settle L) |
| Track switch / d≈0 | Single C-type strike, no A or B | −10 to −16 dB re a d=0.33 gesture | I, L (settle-dominated seeks) |

**Excitation (per strike).** Use a raised-cosine force pulse F(t) = 1 − cos(2πt/T) with **T = 0.06–0.25 ms**, set as T = 0.25 − 0.15·√d ms so harder, longer seeks sound brighter. Only duration matters perceptually, not exact shape ([van den Doel & Pai](https://persianney.com/kvdoelcsubc/publications/modalpaper.pdf)). Contact time sets the attack's spectral centroid, which listeners hear as hardness ([Avanzini & Rocchesso](https://air.unimi.it/retrieve/dfa8b99e-f744-748b-e053-3a05fe0a3a96/avanzini_smc04_finalsub.pdf)). Follow the pulse with **2–4 micro-bounce pulses** at 0.15–0.8 ms spacing (±20%), each 0.3–0.5× the previous, imitating the micro-collisions of hard impacts ([van den Doel & Pai](https://persianney.com/kvdoelcsubc/publications/modalpaper.pdf)). Add a **contact-grit** noise burst of 0.2–0.5 ms, highpassed at about 3 kHz, at −10 to −18 dB re the pulse, to give the "tk" on top of the ring (I). ClatterDrive's fast-minus-slow exponential pulse (τ_fast 0.22–0.34 ms, τ_slow 1.35–2.6 ms) is an equivalent alternative ([ClatterDrive source](https://github.com/alexyorke/clatterdrive)). Reuse the idea only: the repository has no license, so its code cannot be copied.

**Modal bank.** Implement each mode as y[n] = 2r·cos(ω)·y[n−1] − r²·y[n−2] + g·x[n], with r = exp(−πf/(Q·fs)) and g = a·sin(ω), so that the impulse response peaks at amplitude a. Specify modes by initial amplitude rather than by bandpass peak gain, because a 0 dB-peak RBJ bandpass makes high-Q modes start quieter and confuses tuning. The default voice below is a "2005 desktop 7200 rpm" drive built from the CC0 Maxtor DM10 and WD1600JD and cross-checked against the BY-SA Seagate clip.

Two practical notes on the bed (inference). Laptop speakers reproduce little below about 200–300 Hz, so on built-in output a bed is carried by its whine tones and air noise, not the 120 Hz fundamental. For the same reason, "weight" belongs in the 0.4–1.4 kHz modes, not in the thump. The architecture review's concern also still applies: a continuous bed keeps the audio engine awake. Tie the bed to a virtual spin-down after a standby timeout, then pause the engine.

**Variation and loudness.** Per rendered variant, apply global frequency ±1%, per-mode frequency ±0.3%, per-mode Q ±15%, per-mode gain ±3 dB, pulse T ±30%, randomized bounce count and spacing, sub-event timing ±10%, and a new noise seed. Play variants shuffled without immediate repeats (I; game practice warns that two variants become monotonous, per [Uppsala](https://babel.speldesign.uu.se/2018/03/01/2018-03-01-maximizing-your-audio-budget-using-pitch-variation/)). **Delete per-buffer peak normalization.** Render every buffer with its physical gains, then compute one scale factor for the whole bank so that a nominal d = 0.33 gesture peaks at about −12 dBFS. At playback, apply a distance law L(d) = −8 + 8·√d dB re a full stroke, an 8 dB span within the 6–10 dB target, plus ±1.5–2 dB jitter. Put a gentle soft limiter at about −1 dBFS on the main mixer for dense overlaps. A small AAM/quiet knob falls out of the physics: multiply pulse T by 2–4 (0.5–1 ms), drop the gesture by 6 dB, and lengthen T(d) by 10–30%, without touching the mode frequencies ([Wikipedia: AAM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Automatic_acoustic_management); [Tom's Hardware](https://www.tomshardware.com/reviews/terabyte-hitachi-acoustic,2084.html)).

## Redesign part 2: a virtual head turns 30 Hz counters into rhythm

Whirr cannot see LBAs, only per-disk read and write op and byte deltas every ~33 ms. The lesson from 86Box is that rhythm emerges from simulated mechanics ([86Box hdd.c](https://github.com/86Box/86Box/blob/master/src/disk/hdd.c)). Whirr should therefore run a small virtual drive whose state is a head position x ∈ [0, 1], a "head free at" time t_free, the time of the last activity, a park flag, and a sweep direction. Each tick's counter excess becomes requests. Each request gets a distance from a locality chain, and the head timeline decides when its gesture sounds. Every rule below is an inference built on the cited numbers. Tune them by ear and validate them with the harness in the final section.

| Step | Rule (defaults for the 2005/2010s 7200 rpm preset) |
|---|---|
| 1. Floor and activity | Keep v2's adaptive floor (fast fall 3 s, slow rise 60 s, capped), but track per-tick mean μ and spread σ of ops during quiet periods. A tick is **active** if ops_tick > μ + max(3σ, 5 ops). Use the **per-tick excess** for events. Keep the 0.3 s EMA only for floor learning and the global "intensity" used for preset blending |
| 2. Classify the tick | b = excess bytes / excess ops. b ≤ 32 KiB → **random** (p_seek 0.8–1.0). 32–256 KiB → **mixed** (0.3–0.6). ≥ 256 KiB → **sequential** (0.02–0.1, plus track-switch ticks). Large reads and writes in the same tick → **copy**. Write-only active tick after ≥ 0.5 s quiet → **flush** |
| 3. Requests | Demand rate D = R_max·(1 − exp(−e/E_sat)), with e = excess ops/s × p_seek. E_sat about 1,500–3,000 ops/s (v2 used 3,000). R_max = the preset's random ceiling (79/s at 7200 rpm), raised up to 1.5× when a tick carries ≥ 4 requests (NCQ). Accumulate fractional demand across ticks. The **first request after ≥ 150 ms idle** is emitted immediately (delay ≤ 2 ms) and is always a long seek, the accent that must feel instant |
| 4. Distance (locality chain) | First request after idle: jump to a uniform target. Afterwards, with p_local = 0.7–0.85 (0.6 for random, 0.9 for mixed): Δ ~ Exponential(mean 0.5–2% of stroke), and 30–50% of local moves are Δ = 0 (rotation only: silent, or a track-switch tick). Otherwise jump uniformly. Aim for a mix of 40–60% zero or very short, 25–40% short or medium, and 5–20% long |
| 5. Elevator | When the tick or backlog holds ≥ 4 requests, sort local targets monotonically in the current sweep direction and add one long return seek at the end of the sweep |
| 6. Seek time | T(d) = t_tt + (t_avg − t_tt)·√(d/0.33) for d < 0.33, and t_avg + (t_full − t_avg)·(d − 0.33)/0.67 beyond. At 7200 rpm (1.0 / 8.5 / 21 ms) this gives 1.6 ms at d = 0.002, 2.3 at 0.01, 3.6 at 0.04, 5.5 at 0.12, 8.5 at 0.33, 13.5 at 0.6 and 21 at 1.0. Use these as the 8 pre-render bins (plus d = 0) |
| 7. Placement | start = max(t_free, tick_start + small arrival jitter). t_free = start + T(d) + t_rot + t_xfer. Random: t_rot ~ U(0, T_rev), with T_rev = 8.33 ms. Near-sequential: t_rot = T_rev − U(0, 1 ms), which quantizes gaps to revolutions. t_xfer = bytes/op ÷ 100–150 MB/s. No onset closer than t_tt to the previous one |
| 8. Budget and backlog | Requests that would push t_free more than **120 ms** beyond now are dropped. Saturation then sounds like an even, continuous grind instead of a growing, lagging queue |
| 9. Accent | Gesture bin = nearest d bin. Level L(d) as in the synthesis spec. Write seeks are 10% longer (L: 9.5 vs 8.5 ms average) |
| 10. Workload textures | **Sequential:** track-switch ticks at min(virtual throughput ÷ ~1 MiB per cylinder, 1 per revolution), −18 to −26 dB, ±10% jitter so they never form a steady buzz, plus occasional real seeks. **Copy:** alternate long seek pairs every chunk (2–8 MB ÷ 100–150 MB/s ≈ 15–80 ms), with track ticks between. **Flush:** long seek in, 2–6 elevator-sorted short seeks, long seek out |
| 11. Idle | Optional ambient ticks: ticks with ops > 0 but below threshold trigger a single short seek with a probability tuned to roughly one per 3–10 s. Park after T_park (8 s in the "Green" preset, off elsewhere): full-stroke gesture plus a ramp-contact strike +3 to +6 dB re a long seek (Seagate describes parking as a single louder "hard click"). Next request after a park: load click, then a 10–20 ms delay before the first seek. Vintage: optional recalibration sweep every N minutes (no published interval). Standby: spin-down after a configurable timeout, then pause the engine |

These rules fix the measured timing defects. The refractory spacing of T(d) + t_rot removes the impossible sub-millisecond gaps. At 7200 rpm a fully random stream then has gaps no shorter than t_tt (1 ms), a mean of about 12.7 ms (8.5 ms seek plus 4.17 ms rotation), and only a small share under 5 ms, consistent with the measured 5 ms p10 (derived). Locality and the elevator turn bursts into runs of soft ticks punctuated by louder accents, which reproduces the measured gap between fine bursts (43/s) and merged seek units (12.9/s). Bursts start on the tick where they happen instead of 0.3 s later. Saturation caps at the mechanical ceiling instead of a smoothed curve. One honest limitation is that the macOS counters sit below the buffer cache and record SSD-scale op counts. The mapping in step 3 is therefore a deliberate compression of a 1,000–10,000 IOPS device into an 80 IOPS fiction, and E_sat is the main knob that sets how "busy" an ordinary Mac sounds.

## Conclusion

Neither the frequencies nor the bass caused the failure. v2's centre of mass at 2.5–5 kHz is right for many drives, and v1's low body exists in half the recordings. What failed was time. v2's kHz bands stop ringing in about 5 ms when real modes ring for 25–50 ms. v1 sounded its low energy at the instant of the click instead of 6–14 ms later and 5–16 dB down. The rhythm then smoothed away the very bursts that the 30 Hz counters preserve. The redesign is therefore mostly about getting durations right: modal Q, burst spacing from the seek curve, and gaps from rotation. Each of these can be measured directly against CC0 recordings, which makes Whirr's realism testable rather than a matter of taste.

Two open questions remain worth recording a real drive for. The first is whether the mid-seek current reversal is audible, which decides whether strike B stays. The second is how much of the 190–300 Hz thump belongs to the drive rather than the desk. A single 7200 rpm drive, recorded once bare, once on a desk and once in a case under fio random, sequential and copy loads while iostat logs at 30 Hz, would settle both. It would also give Whirr something no prior HDD-sound project has had: timing rules validated against paired counter traces and audio.
