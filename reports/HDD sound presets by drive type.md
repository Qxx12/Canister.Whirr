# Recordings Redraw Whirr's Hard-Drive Sound Lineup

Whirr should offer **twelve drive types** in its picker, and treat mounting (bare on a desk, closed tower, vented tower, laptop, USB shell) as a **second, independent menu**. Eight of the twelve can ship on today's `DrivePreset` engine with voices measured from openly licensed recordings: vintage1993, desktop2000, desktop2005, thunk2007, quiet2010s (still the default), laptop2007, server10k and server15k. Four need new engine features. **stepper1987** needs an impulse-train gesture, because a real 1987 stepper seek turned out to be 4–12 discrete knocks about 5.5 ms apart, a "brrrt", not the "fweep" chirp predicted from datasheets. **woody2000** needs a sustained writing-rumble layer. **helium2020** needs a periodic idle tick like WD's roughly 5 s Preventive Wear Leveling sweep, and its voice is wholly estimated because no openly licensed helium recording exists. **failing** needs a metronomic scheduler, because what marks a failing drive is regular timing more than timbre. The new measurements also correct existing code. `laptop2005`'s "desktop modes × 1.3" puts its strongest mode at 2.25 kHz, but a real 2.5-inch seek tick peaks at **6.38 kHz** with nothing event-locked between 0.5 and 2 kHz. The decay-derived **Q of server and 2007-era resonances is 150–300 in the kHz range and 13–26 for low thumps**, far above earlier window-limited estimates. That makes thunk2007's thump a Q≈22 boom, not a Q≈4.5 knock. The main caveats are that every measured number comes from lossy, uncalibrated close-mic previews, and that Whirr plays only on the Mac's built-in speaker. That speaker reproduces little of the 60–250 Hz content that gives thunk2007, woody2000 and helium2020 their identity.

## Twelve drive types survive the evidence, and mounting is a second menu

The lineup below follows one rule: a drive type earns a slot only if it sounds structurally different, not just a few hundred hertz higher or lower. "Measured" means the research team's own analysis of CC0 or public-domain recordings: decay-derived mode lists, band envelopes and onset statistics. "Estimated" means the value comes from datasheets, user reports or physics. Display names avoid brand names; the reference drive can go in documentation.

Five candidates were considered and deferred. A **1988–91 full-height 5.25-inch voice-coil** preset has datasheet timing but no openly licensed recording, so its voice would be a guess. Its only distinct feature, thermal-recalibration sweeps, fits better as an option on vintage1993. **1.8-inch iPod and 1-inch Microdrive** drives have actuator modes pushed above about 7 kHz ([IBM Hot Chips 13](https://old.hotchips.org/wp-content/uploads/hc_archives/hc13/3_Tue/18ibm-microdrive.pdf)) and no healthy recording, so they would be a fainter laptop2007. **Mach.2 dual-actuator** drives were reported quieter than single-actuator 20 TB drives in one informal test ([NASCompares](https://nascompares.com/review/seagate-exos-mach-2-2x18-hard-drive-review-should-you-buy/)), and would need two virtual heads. **Separate Seagate and Toshiba helium variants** rest on user reports alone. A **USB portable** is a 2.5-inch laptop drive in a different shell, so it belongs on the mounting menu.

Mounting is a separate axis because the same drive changes character with what it is bolted to. SPCR found a Momentus 5400.2's seeks "completely inaudible" on soft foam but "clearly audible" on a vibration box ([SPCR p.2, Wayback](https://web.archive.org/web/2007/http://www.silentpcreview.com/article264-page2.html)). A hard-mounted desktop drive measured about 12 dBA louder on seeks than a suspended one ([HWCooling](https://www.hwcooling.net/en/?p=7404)). Today `desktop2005InTower` and `desktop2005InVentedTower` are separate presets that copy `desktop2005` and swap in an `Enclosure` (`Sources/Whirr/SoundBank.swift`). That will not scale to twelve drives. The picker should offer a drive menu plus a mounting menu, as below. (Whirr first filtered the mounting menu by form factor; it now lets any drive go in any mounting and only notes the pairings no real computer had.) Every enclosure value in `Enclosure.swift` is inferred from physics, not measured, so a tower or laptop mounting is always an estimated layer on top of a measured drive.

## Old drives knock in trains, clack late, or rumble while writing

### A stepper seek is a fixed-rate "brrrt", not a "fweep"

Stepper drives position their heads open-loop. In the ST-506/ST-412 a stepper motor drives a band actuator, and "each step pulse will cause the heads to move either 1 track in or 1 track out" ([Seagate ST506/412 OEM manual](https://www.minuszerodegrees.net/manuals/Seagate/Seagate%20ST506%20ST412%20-%20OEM%20Manual.pdf)). The datasheets describe slow drives with heavy settling. The ST-412 is rated 3/85/205 ms track-to-track/average/full stroke with a 15 ms settle. The ST-225 is rated about 20/65/150 ms with a 20 ms settle ([ST225 OEM manual](https://www.minuszerodegrees.net/manuals/Seagate/Seagate%20ST225%20-%20OEM%20Manual%20-%20Oct85.pdf)). The ST-277N is rated **8/40/95 ms maximum at 3600 rpm over 820 cylinders** ([ST251N/ST277N manual](https://ftp.mirrorservice.org/sites/www.bitsavers.org/pdf/seagate/scsi/36034-001B_ST251N_ST277N_Product_Manual_Mar87.pdf)). From the 3 ms minimum step period and the buffered-seek acceleration these manuals describe, one would predict a 333 Hz buzz or a pitch-swept kHz chirp. Enthusiasts call it "fweep fweep fweep" ([Hacker News](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=44538819)).

The only openly licensed stepper recording disagrees. It is a CC0 6.5-minute capture of a 1987 ST-277N being powered up, used and powered down ([Freesound 475216](https://freesound.org/people/jodybruchon/sounds/475216/)). In it, a seek is a **train of discrete impulses about 5.5 ms apart (174–182 per second), starting at full rate with no ramp-up and no kHz pitch sweep**. Typical seeks have six impulses with a fixed core of 5.5, 5.5, 5.5, 6.5 and 7.0 ms (38 ms in total). Long seeks have 8–10 impulses (60–66 ms) ending in a slowing tail of 10.5 and 13.5 ms. Each impulse re-strikes the same modes, and spectra repeat within ±1 dB from seek to seek. Those modes are a moderately damped cluster at **1428, 1385, 915 and 1012 Hz (Q≈22–40)**, with weaker ones at 845, 1304, 1077, 709 and 343 Hz. The perceived pitch is therefore the impulse rate, about 174 Hz falling to 70–95 Hz in the tail, carried on a 1.4 kHz ring. Single-track steps are different: lone, darker "toks" weighted to **573 and 458 Hz**, which recur every **300–350 ms** during sequential reads. That spacing is about 19 revolutions, consistent with a six-head cylinder read at interleave 3 (inference). Of 1,902 gestures in the use passage, 1,208 were single impulses and only 231 had four or more, at 0.77 per second ([475216](https://freesound.org/people/jodybruchon/sounds/475216/)).

The impulses cannot be tracks. Crossing 820 tracks in 95 ms needs 9–10 kHz stepping, while the recording shows only 6–10 impulses per seek. Each audible knock is more likely a coarse motor phase cycle or carriage rocking (inference). The engine therefore needs a gesture whose impulse count comes from seek time: n = clamp(round(T(d) / 6 ms), 1, 12) gives 1–2 impulses for track-to-track, about 7 for an average seek and 12 for a full stroke. Intervals come from the measured table with ±0.5 ms jitter. An optional 0.5–0.57 kHz "landing" knock follows 0–45 ms after the **last** impulse, which means the `Thump` delay must be measurable from the end of the gesture rather than the start. Seek time for steppers is close to linear in distance with a large intercept (about 22 ms + 0.21 ms per track on the ST-225, derived from its datasheet), so the √d law in `seekTime` fits poorly. The recording also gives a full power-up and power-down script, all measured. The spindle ramps about 7 s to 60 Hz with a 7.7% overshoot. About 6 s of continuous 5.75 ms impulse buzz follows, then a 180–250 ms recalibration rattle of 30–42 impulses, then normal activity. Power-down is about 2.4 s of stepping during spin-down (the auto-park), the loudest event in the file at +6–7 dB over idle in 0.5 s RMS. Two things should be left out: a 213 Hz tone comb that is not locked to the spindle, and 12–15 kHz tone bursts after each seek. Both are probably the test bench or electronics. The caveats are real. This is one drive with a loud tonal bed, and its seeks rise only +3.2 dB broadband over idle, so the mic probably favoured the spindle. Drives that ramp their step rate under buffered-seek firmware, such as the ST-412 and ST-225, might still chirp. Still, the "brrrt" is the only stepper sound anyone has measured, and 86Box cannot make it: it plays one single-track sample per cylinder change even for its stepper profile ([86Box hdd_audio.c](https://github.com/86Box/86Box/blob/master/src/disk/hdd_audio.c)).

### Early voice-coil drives clack slowly over a late knock

**vintage1993** keeps its current voice, which was measured from a CC0 Conner CFS420A "seek test" ([Freesound 155882](https://freesound.org/people/Klerrp/sounds/155882/)). Its largest seek excess is at 264 Hz, followed by 1.89 kHz (−9 dB), 764 Hz (−10) and 2.72 kHz (−12). Its 150–400 Hz band peaks 7 ms after onset and falls −10 dB by 11 ms and −20 dB by 15 ms, against a 60 Hz idle line and whine tones at 1080, 2004 and 3466 Hz. Rotary voice-coil drives of this class latch the actuator against the inner stop at power-down, run startup diagnostics, and are rated at no more than **40 dBA at 1 m** ([Conner CP3104 manual](https://ftp.mirrorservice.org/sites/www.bitsavers.org/pdf/conner/disk/00501-023_Conner_CP3104_Product_Manual_199008.pdf)). That is notably quieter than the ST-412's 50 dBA rating, though the stepper's measurement distance is not given. Two optional events would add period character. The first is a startup seek test: the Conner's runs strictly periodic at a 58 ms interval, and Quantum drives of the era make a "familiar click pattern" at spin-up ([downtowndougbrown.com](https://downtowndougbrown.com/2025/03/the-gooey-rubber-thats-slowly-ruining-old-hard-drives)). The second is a thermal-recalibration sweep of about one second, which dedicated-servo drives ran "every 20 or 30 minutes" ([comp.periphs.scsi, 1993](https://discmaster.textfiles.com/view/28249/NN_1993_3.iso/spool/comp/periphs/scsi/6705)). The Conner has embedded servo, so it would run TCAL only at power-on, and the sweep's content is undocumented.

### The two 2000-era drives sound nothing alike

**desktop2000** keeps the Fujitsu MPF3102AT voice. Excess peaks sit at 888, 1378 and 2659 Hz, with 41% of A-weighted excess in 300 Hz–1 kHz, a 3 ms first −10 dB drop and a median T60 of about 25 ms ([Freesound 166638](https://freesound.org/people/Klerrp/sounds/166638/)). **woody2000**, from the CC0 Samsung SV1022D, is the more interesting case, because the new analysis overturns how that drive was first described. The earlier reading put its "voice" at 194, 458 and 678 Hz. In fact its **event-locked click is a short, damped 3.4–4.9 kHz tick, strongest at 4064 Hz with decay-derived Q≈39–75 (T60 21–49 ms)**. The 196, 353, 455 and 684 Hz components rise only +2–3 dB at each onset, while the whole writing passage raises the 150–300 Hz band +26 dB over idle ([Freesound 167420](https://freesound.org/people/Klerrp/sounds/167420/)). The "woody" sound is a **sustained, re-excited writing rumble** under ticks that arrive at about 12 gestures per second, not a per-seek resonance. Built from per-seek modes alone, woody2000 would become a dry 4 kHz tick close to quiet2010s. It needs an activity layer: band-limited noise or a re-struck low resonator bank with humps at 187–207 and 332–361 Hz, gated by write activity. Its low-mode Q values are only lower bounds (Q ≳ 9 at 196 Hz, ≳ 16 at 353 Hz), because events arrive 30 ms apart and the modes never fully decay. Timing for both 2000 presets (5400 rpm, about 2.5/11.5/22 ms) is an estimate from 86Box era presets, not a datasheet.

## Modern desktops hold up; laptops and servers live in the high kilohertz

### desktop2005, thunk2007 and quiet2010s span the 2005–2012 desktop

**desktop2005** stays as built. Its voice comes from a Maxtor DiamondMax 10 (1728 Hz strongest, 291 Hz −5 dB, 991/1965 Hz −8) ([Freesound 167422](https://freesound.org/people/Klerrp/sounds/167422/)) and a WD1600JD (1114, 2191, 5712, 2740 and 5469 Hz within 6 dB) ([Freesound 166898](https://freesound.org/people/Klerrp/sounds/166898/)). Its timing is from datasheets: **1.0 ms track-to-track, 8.5 ms average, 4.16 ms latency** ([Seagate 7200.12 manual](https://www.seagate.com/staticfiles/support/disc/manuals/desktop/Barracuda%207200.12/100529369h.pdf)) and a **21 ms full stroke** ([WD datasheet](https://images10.newegg.com/UploadFilesForNewegg/itemintelligence/WD/22-144-701/WD2500KS.pdf)). Together these cap random I/O at about 79 seeks/s. **quiet2010s**, the right default, stays at 3.75–3.93 kHz with a 6.07 kHz partial at −11 dB ([Freesound 166639](https://freesound.org/people/Klerrp/sounds/166639/), [166641](https://freesound.org/people/Klerrp/sounds/166641/)). The WD Green behind it parks its heads after **8 s** idle by default ([NETGEAR community](https://community.netgear.com/t5/ReadyNAS-Hardware-Compatibility/WD-Red-drives-park-their-heads-like-WD-Green-drives/td-p/869565)), so a head-park click is a natural optional event.

**thunk2007** is the most distinct new desktop voice. In the CC0 Samsung T166, the **strongest component is a 207 Hz thump, +40 dB over idle, peaking about 20 ms after onset and decaying with T60 213–280 ms (Q≈20–26)**. A 377 Hz knock follows at −4 dB (Q≈28–30), while the kHz modes at 4.5–5.4 kHz sit 21–29 dB below the thump with Q 100–300 ([Freesound 167421](https://freesound.org/people/Klerrp/sounds/167421/)). Unweighted, 87% of its excess energy is below 300 Hz. The drive gestures only about 4 times per second, with each gesture lasting 45 ms (median). That is the "thunk": slow, heavy and booming. The current engine can render it: the measured block makes the 377 Hz knock the 0 dB mode and uses `Thump(hz: 207, q: 22, db: 4, delay: 0.012...0.020)`. The risk is playback. Whirr plays only on the built-in speaker (repo commit 14a7c12), and laptop speakers reproduce little below about 200–300 Hz (inference). On the built-in speaker the 207 Hz thump will mostly vanish, and the identity will rest on the 377 and 474–576 Hz modes and the slow rhythm. It should be auditioned there before it ships.

### Laptop drives tick at 6.4 kHz, so laptop2005's scaling is wrong

`laptop2005` was built on an untested assumption: no 2.5-inch recording was available, so it scales `desktop2005`'s modes up 30% for a lighter actuator (`SoundBank.swift`). That puts its strongest modes at 2249, 3419 and 2847 Hz, with 1448/1288 Hz "weight". The CC0 Fujitsu MHV2200BT (2007, 4200 rpm) shows the real thing ([Freesound 143917](https://freesound.org/people/Klerrp/sounds/143917/)). It is a faint, very short tick whose **dominant mode is 6382 Hz (Q≈54), with 6189 and 6753 Hz partials and a weak 2766/2879 Hz pair at −6/−11 dB (Q≈40–70)**. It falls −10 dB within about 5 ms, and nothing event-locked appears between 478 Hz and 2.08 kHz above −13.6 dB. That is **1.7× quiet2010s's strongest mode and 2.8× laptop2005's**. The tick rises +15.5 dB (median) in the 3–16 kHz band, but only **+3.5 dB broadband** over the spindle and air hiss. It arrives at about 5.5 gestures per second, in clusters of 2–5 over 20–200 ms. A 5400 rpm Toshiba MK5055GSX recorded the same way showed **no detectable seeks at all** while writing sequentially ([Freesound 143269](https://freesound.org/people/Klerrp/sounds/143269/)). Both drives carry strong spindle cues: 70.0 Hz plus tones at 10×, 12× and 16× rotation (700, 840 and 1122 Hz) on the Fujitsu, and 90 Hz plus 1080 and 2160 Hz on the Toshiba. This matches SPCR's finding that 2.5-inch airborne seeks sit only 0–3 dBA over idle and that most of what listeners hear is "vibration-induced" ([SPCR p.3, Wayback](https://web.archive.org/web/2007/http://www.silentpcreview.com/article264-page3.html)). A contact-mic recording of a USB portable supports the same split: its seek is a 314–390 Hz structure-borne knock plus a 5.1–5.7 kHz tick ([Freesound 401271](https://freesound.org/people/eardeer/sounds/401271/)).

The fix, **laptop2007**, has four parts. First, replace the bank with the measured 6.38/6.19/2.77/6.75/2.88 kHz list. Second, drop every sub-2 kHz mode and let `Enclosure.laptopOnDesk` carry the low end. Third, raise `lowPassHz` from 9.5 to about 12 kHz so the 9.28 kHz partial survives. Fourth, keep the tick quiet; the recording sets it against a 70 Hz-plus-hiss bed, which Whirr leaves out (see the revision section). Timing is datasheet-class but not primary: **4200 rpm, 1.5 ms track-to-track, 14 ms read seek, 22 ms full stroke, 7.14 ms latency** come from an unverified listing ([Icecat MHV2200BT](https://icecat.co.uk/prefere/gb/p/fujitsu/mhv2200bt/MHV2200BT-693264.html)). They are consistent with Seagate's Momentus 5400.6 (1 ms track-to-track, 14 ms average, 2.4/2.6 B idle/seek) ([Seagate manual](https://www.seagate.com/staticfiles/support/disc/manuals/notebook/momentus/5400.6%20(Wyatt)/100528359e.pdf)), and they give a ceiling of about 47 seeks/s. Laptop drives also park on a ramp whenever they idle, which is the sound behind the classic "tick tick tick" complaints and load-cycle counts of 15,539 in one week ([ThinkWiki](https://www.thinkwiki.org/wiki/Problem_with_hard_drive_clicking)). One Fujitsu waited "10-15 seconds after a seek before unloading" ([SPCR p.3](https://web.archive.org/web/2007/http://www.silentpcreview.com/article264-page3.html)). The Fujitsu recording includes measured unload clicks: 17 and 46 ms long, +32–34 dB in 3–16 kHz, with peaks at 6.3, 8.2, 7.2 and 9.2 kHz. Those are a ready template for a ramp-unload event. Apple's Sudden Motion Sensor parks the heads on a jolt and makes "a clicking noise" ([Wikipedia: Sudden Motion Sensor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sudden_Motion_Sensor)), which could become an easter egg tied to trackpad thumps. A USB shell mounting can borrow the emergency-park click from the contact-mic recording: about 50 ms, roughly 27–30 dB above a seek, peaking at 9–11 kHz. That recording is CC BY 4.0, which requires attribution only if its audio ships, and it clipped, so the level is a lower bound.

### Server drives ring at Q 150–300 over a tonal whine

Server drives get two slots because the two CC0 recordings differ more than their rpm suggests. Decay fits show the **Maxtor Atlas 10K V ringing at 4.6–6.5 kHz with Q≈170–280 (T60 75–160 ms)**, strongest at 6481 and 5984 Hz. A second group at 1.0–2.3 kHz, led by 2021 Hz at −2.4 dB, **peaks about 10 ms after the first**, and a faint 129–199 Hz swell peaks at 21–24 ms ([Freesound 182366](https://freesound.org/people/Klerrp/sounds/182366/)). It is the brightest healthy drive in the set, with 23% of A-weighted seek energy above 5 kHz. Its idle is a 166.8 Hz fundamental plus a **14,004 Hz whine (84× rotation)** in a comb spaced every 500 Hz from 10 to 15.5 kHz. Its timing is from the Maxtor manual: **average read seek under 4.0–4.4 ms, full stroke 11 ms, 0.3/0.5 ms head switch, 3 ms latency** ([Maxtor 10K V manual p.52](https://www.manualsdir.com/manuals/123661/maxtor-10k-v.html?page=52)). That gives a ceiling of about 139 seeks/s. The **Fujitsu MBA3300RC (15k)** is not brighter but denser. It centres on **3604 Hz (Q≈186) with a 1.1–1.3 kHz body and high-Q 4.3–6.3 kHz partials**, has no thump, and idles on a 250.0 Hz fundamental plus a 5997 Hz whine (24× rotation). Under sustained writing it becomes a continuous roar 12–13 dB above idle with only about 6 dB of crest, peaking at 533 Hz and 1.0–1.3 kHz ([Freesound 182364](https://freesound.org/people/Klerrp/sounds/182364/)). Its timing comes from the same class of drive from another vendor, Seagate's Cheetah 15K.7: **3.4/3.9 ms average read/write, 0.2/0.44 ms track-to-track, 6.6/7.4 ms full stroke, 2.0 ms latency** ([Cheetah 15K.7 manual](https://archive.org/download/manualsonline-id-3ef39731-d1a1-4222-8c31-490b7fa44f7e/3ef39731-d1a1-4222-8c31-490b7fa44f7e.pdf)). That gives about 185 seeks/s. Fujitsu's own track-to-track figure (0.2/0.4 ms) agrees.

Two things set servers apart beyond their modes. First, **the bed dominates**. Sound power rises only about 0.3–0.4 B from idle to seeking: 3.4 to 3.8 B on the Cheetah, and 3.16–3.67 to 3.60–3.96 B on the Atlas ([Maxtor 10K V manual p.55](https://www.manualsdir.com/manuals/123661/maxtor-10k-v.html?page=55)). In a recording, a server preset without its spindle bed is only half the sound. Whirr deliberately leaves the bed out, because it plays only sound that follows disk activity (see the revision section), so the server identity has to come from the ring, the density and the heavy-write roar. Second, with Q above 200 and gestures 20–80 ms apart, the modes never fully decay during busy periods. The result is a shimmering, overlapping texture that the current engine produces as long as Q is set high. The Cheetah also documents an idle behaviour Whirr can copy: heads "relocated every 60 seconds to a random location" in the outer quarter of the stroke.

## Helium and failing drives are defined by rhythm more than timbre

### helium2020 is the one estimated voice

Helium drives are the most common hard drives sold today, so the lineup needs one, but **no openly licensed, model-labelled recording of any helium drive exists**. The only PWL recording found is on an unlicensed host ([Tom's Hardware forum](https://forums.tomshardware.com/threads/new-ultrastar-10tb-hdd-annoying-pwl-noise-what-now-buy-wd-purple-pro-instead-noise-example-included.3798784)). The design therefore rests on datasheets and user reports. The datasheets give a near-silent idle against loud seeks. The WD Ultrastar DC HC550 is rated **2.0 B idle and 3.6 B operating** and publishes no seek times at all ([WD HC550 data sheet](https://documents.westerndigital.com/content/dam/doc-library/en_us/assets/public/western-digital/product/data-center-drives/ultrastar-dc-hc500-series/data-sheet-ultrastar-dc-hc550.pdf)). The Seagate Exos X18 is rated 2.8/3.2 B and puts 18 heads on one actuator ([Exos X18 manual](https://www.seagate.com/www-content/product-content/enterprise-hdd-fam/exos-x18/_shared/en-us/docs/100865854a.pdf)). Toshiba's MG10 is rated 20/32 dB sound power ([Toshiba MG10 overview](https://toshiba-semicon-storage.com/content/dam/toshiba-ss-v3/master/en/storage/product/data-center-enterprise/MG10-Product-Overview_rev.4.pdf)). That idle-to-seek jump of 1.2–1.6 B, against about 0.4 B for servers, is the defining contrast: each seek stands out alone against near silence. Helium, at "about 1/7 the density of air", quiets the spin ([WD blog](https://blog.westerndigital.com/helium-hard-drives-explained/)). Users describe the seeks as loud, low clacking that sounds like "old 20MB hard drives" ([Overclockers UK](https://forums.overclockers.co.uk/threads/noisy-seagate-exos-x20s.18976282)), though others hear no difference between air and helium and blame enclosure vibration ([Storj forum](https://forum.storj.io/t/are-helium-filed-hdds-louder/19653)).

The most recognisable helium sound is not a seek under load but **Preventive Wear Leveling**: a single normal-sounding head movement about every 5 s while the drive is idle. Users report it on WD Red Pro, Gold and Ultrastar drives, sometimes once a minute on older builds ([TrueNAS forum](https://www.truenas.com/community/threads/new-wd-red-pros-clicking-every-5-seconds-preventive-wear-leveling-firmware-feature.105866); [WD Community](https://community.wd.com/t/periodic-noise-from-head-movement-wd-black-4tb/183096)). WD publishes no specification for it. helium2020 should therefore be built from estimated parts. Its voice would be a heavier, lower body (about 150–600 Hz) under a 1–4 kHz click, an inference from the larger actuator. Its timing would be 7200 rpm with 4.16 ms latency and an assumed 8.5 ms average seek. The **PWL scheduler** would play a mid-length seek every 5.0 ± 0.3 s while the host is idle, suppressed during I/O. The preset should be labelled "estimated" in the picker, and the cheapest upgrade is to record an owned WD Red Pro or Exos and release the recording as CC0. Because the body sits largely below what the built-in speaker reproduces, the PWL rhythm will carry this preset's identity more than its timbre.

### failing is a rhythm generator with measured voices

Healthy drives seek irregularly, with heavy-tailed gaps. Failing drives are metronomic, and that regularity is what a listener recognises. The CC0 WD102AA read-retry clip ticks at **47 bursts/s with a median 16 ms interval (a 60 Hz buzz)**. The bursts group into 51 ms gestures and those into about 260 ms groups about 367 ms apart, in episodes of about 2 s that recur about every 5 s. Its voice is a 280 Hz knock with no delay plus 2098 Hz (Q≈92) and 1.1–1.5 kHz modes ([Commons WD102AA](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:WD102AA_Scratched_area_read_retries.ogg)). The public-domain "click of death" repeats a **509 ms cycle of two clicks 162 ms apart** in runs of about 5 s. Each click is a sharp 4.5–5.6 kHz crack (Q≈30–45) over a long 1.02–1.11 kHz ring, peaking +42 dB over idle in 1–5 kHz ([Commons WD click of death](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:WD_bad_heads_click_of_death.ogg)). The IBM Deskstar failure clip is CC BY-SA 3.0, so it is excluded from anything that ships ([Commons Deskstar](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Sound_of_defective_IBM_Deskstar.ogg)). The engine needs a scheduler that ignores the virtual head and emits these fixed patterns, triggered by I/O so the drive still sounds tied to the Mac. One product risk is that a convincing click of death from a Mac's own speaker could alarm a user who forgot the setting was on. The picker should label it plainly and never make it the default.

## Five corrections to the current code, and seven engine features in build order

The new analysis changes numbers already in `SoundBank.swift` and in the earlier synthesis guidance. The most important correction is a method finding. Spectral −3 dB widths in 20–30 ms windows **cap measurable Q at about f/50–72**: Q≈36 at 2.6 kHz and Q≈4 at 280 Hz. The earlier "Q≈15–35" and "thump Q≈4–5" were largely window limits. Fitting the decay of a Gaussian narrowband envelope gives far higher values wherever the bed is quiet enough to see the tail ([Freesound 182366](https://freesound.org/people/Klerrp/sounds/182366/), [182364](https://freesound.org/people/Klerrp/sounds/182364/), [167421](https://freesound.org/people/Klerrp/sounds/167421/)). These decay Q values include any continuing excitation, such as settle and track-following, so they are upper bounds on the free ring.

Two smaller fixes belong in the tooling. The inherited analysis scripts (`hdd_batch.py`, `preset_measure.py`, `longenv.py`, `clusters.py`) index 44-sample frames as if they were 1 ms long. Hand-picked ranges therefore drift by 0.23%, which is 0.9 s at 390 s. Durations are unaffected, but range masks on long files are not, and `preset_measure2.py` already maps time with the true frame rate. The earlier estimate that the Atlas has 41% of its energy above 5 kHz falls to 23% with cleaner idle ranges. Range choice moves band shares by tens of percent, so shares should be compared only within one analysis pass.

## Revision after the first listen: a body for every click, and no constant noise

The first listen (2026-11-25) raised two problems with the samples.

**The clicks lacked low-mid body.** Every voice above was fitted to close-miked recordings. A microphone a few centimetres from a drive picks up the small, fast-ringing actuator modes much more strongly than a listener at desk distance would. Small and phone microphones also roll off below a few hundred hertz. A listener in a room hears relatively more of the large plates (the top cover and the base casting) and of reflections from the desk and walls. So fitting to recordings made every click "flat": all kilohertz ring, no knock. The prototype now adds a **body layer** to each preset: three plate modes at f, 1.6f and 2.4f, with Q 6–14. They are driven by a softened copy of each strike, because the casting passes only the low part of the jolt. The arm's push-pull reaction also drives them, so short seeks knock slightly deeper.

The first-mode frequencies come from the clamped-plate formula f ≈ (λ²/2πa²)·√(D/ρh) for each form factor:

Levels were solved, not guessed. Each body was set so that a 1/3-stroke gesture carries about as much 250–1200 Hz energy as 1.5–8 kHz energy. The target is +2 dB for the heavy old drives (vintage1993, woody2000, helium2020), 0 dB for desktop2005, and −3 dB for the small, quiet or bright drives (laptop2007, quiet2010s, both servers). Before the change these gaps were −3 to −17 dB. desktop2000, thunk2007, stepper1987 and failing already had low-mid energy from their measured modes, so they got only a light body. To reach the targets, the first body mode sits up to 7 dB above the strongest actuator mode. That is expected: the click is many long, narrow rings, while each plate ring is short and broad. Two smaller changes go with it. Room reflections are now low-passed at 4 kHz, because surfaces absorb the top. The default bass cut drops from 250 to 120 Hz, which no longer trims the body. Every part of the body layer is estimated, and the bench's Body level, pitch and ring knobs exist to tune it by ear.

**Constant running noise is out of scope.** The earlier samples previewed a spindle bed (hum, rotation-locked whine and hiss) for the servers, the laptop, the stepper and vintage1993. Whirr should voice only what follows disk activity, so the bed is gone from the prototype, the samples and the build order. The recordings' bed measurements stay in this report as context. Dynamic layers stay: the write-gated rumble (woody2000, server15k), helium's PWL tick and the failure patterns all start and stop with activity.

## Conclusion

The lineup splits into drives whose identity lives in **structure and rhythm**: the stepper's 174 Hz knock train, the laptop's tiny tick, the server's never-decaying ring, helium's 5 s idle tick, and the failing drive's metronome. The rest are identified by **timbre**, by where a few modes sit. That split matters more than era, because Whirr plays only on a laptop speaker that very likely drops most content below 200–300 Hz. Rhythm and kHz modes survive that speaker. The 207 Hz thunk of thunk2007, the hollow rumble of woody2000 and the heavy body of helium2020 do not. So the "structural" drives are the safest picks to make the picker feel varied, and the bass-defined drives are the ones to audition on the actual hardware before shipping. It also follows that an idle-event scheduler and the activity layers are worth more than further mode tuning, because they change the rhythm and not just the click.

The evidence now covers healthy drives from 1987 to 2012 plus two servers, all from openly licensed recordings. It stops exactly where today's users are, at helium NAS drives, and it never separates a drive from its mounting. One recording session would close both gaps. Record an owned helium drive, idle and under load, once bare, once in a case and once on rubber, while logging Whirr's 30 Hz counters, and release it as CC0. That session would replace helium2020's only estimated voice, settle whether low thumps belong to the drive or the desk, and give the mounting menu its first measured enclosure.
